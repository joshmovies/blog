Let me define something first, because the rest of this post stands on it.

Confidence is certainty about how a task will turn out. When you are sure of the outcome, you are confident. When you are not sure, your mind fills the gap with the worst picture it can find. That gap is what most singers call fear.

Notice what that definition does not say. It does not say a confident singer feels nothing. It says a confident singer is certain about the work.

## The old belief about nerves

Many of us were raised to see nerves as a bad sign. If your hands shake before you sing, you are not ready. If your heart is racing, maybe you are not anointed for it. So singers hide the shaking, pretend to be calm, and then wonder why the first line comes out thin.

That belief is a preference passed down as a fact. In the Handbook I wrote that it is okay to be nervous, but you must learn to speak to yourself in those moments. Nervous and ready can live in the same body.

Think of 2 students walking into a WAEC exam hall. Both have a fast heartbeat. Both are holding their pens a little too tight. One read every night for 3 months. The other started reading on Thursday. From the inside, their nerves feel the same. What separates them is what each one is sure of when the paper lands on the desk.

So leave the nerves alone. Give your mind something it can be certain of.

## The panel of judges

I call the real problem **the panel of judges**.

In the Handbook I described it this way: there is always this feeling that one is in front of a panel of judges, waiting to be validated or rejected. I also wrote that the pressure to impress is the major reason many people get on stage nervous, even after they have rehearsed hard and have their team in place.

Read that again. Even after rehearsal. Even with a good team. The singer was prepared, and the fear still came, because the singer walked out thinking the church was a courtroom and they were the one on trial.

Do we understand this? Nobody in the room called that panel together. It sits in your head. And it gets in through 3 doors.

### 1. You think the service is about you

When you believe the whole outcome of the service rests on your voice, every note becomes evidence for or against you. A cracked note stops being a cracked note. It becomes a verdict.

Paul wrote this to Timothy, a young leader:

> "For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind." (2 Timothy 1:7)

Power. That is your preparation and the Spirit's help working together. Love. That is your attention on the people, instead of on yourself. A sound mind. That is a mind that is not running a trial in the middle of a song.

Fear grows where the singer has made the moment about the singer. Love moves your eyes off yourself and onto the people you are serving. In the Handbook I put it simply: the one whose responsibility it is to make everything happen well is the same person your heart is set to glorify. You can put that weight down.

Watch a good MC at a Nigerian wedding. He is not standing at the microphone wondering whether the guests like him. He is watching the programme, the couple, the band and the time. His attention is on the event, so the event flows. Bring that same attention to Sunday, and the panel loses its seat.

### 2. You are not sure of what you know

Permit me to speak a bit bluntly. Some of the fear is not spiritual at all. Some of it is homework you have not done.

In the Handbook I wrote that a major reason many singers lack confidence is that they don't know enough songs to handle a session, and they don't trust that what they know is enough. If you stand up unsure of the second verse, unsure of the key, unsure of where the song goes after the bridge, your mind is right to worry. It is reporting a real gap.

Picture a woman cooking jollof for her family on a Sunday. She doesn't stand by the pot praying the rice will turn out well. She knows when the tomatoes go in. She knows how her pot behaves. She has done it 100 times, and her family has told her what to adjust. That certainty came from repetition, practice and feedback. Singing works the same way. In my confidence guide I name 5 ingredients: repetition, practice, bonding with your voice, mentorship and feedback. When they are missing from your singing, fear moves into the empty space.

### 3. You have no proof

Most singers can remember every bad performance they have ever had. Ask them for a good one and they go quiet. They have been collecting evidence for the prosecution only.

Research on vocal students points to why this matters. A 2025 study in Frontiers in Psychology looked at how support from teachers and peers relates to performance anxiety in vocal students. One of the main links was self-efficacy, which is your belief that you can do the task. Support lowered anxiety largely by raising that belief. The students were measured at one point in time, so the study shows a connection, not proof of cause. But it fits what the Handbook and the guide both teach. Belief grows from evidence. If you never keep your good takes and never write down what went well, you give that belief nothing to grow on.

## Now let me establish balance

I am not telling you the nerves will disappear once you understand this. They may not. Many experienced singers still feel their heart race before a big session. That is your body getting ready for something that matters.

I am also not saying preparation replaces God. Prayer and preparation work together. You prepare as if it depends on you, then you minister trusting that it doesn't.

And if your fear is so strong that it stops you singing at all, or it spills into the rest of your life, please talk to a counsellor or a doctor.

> **Nerves tell you it matters. The panel of judges tells you it is about you. Only one of them is telling the truth.**

## Do it now

**1. Name your judges (7 minutes, today).** Write down every comment about your voice that still plays in your head before you sing, and next to each one, who said it. Mark each line F (a fact a trained coach would agree with) or P (someone's preference). Cross out the P lines.

**2. Close one gap (20 minutes, before your next rehearsal).** Pick one song you are expected to lead soon. Learn every verse, the key and the order of the song until you can sing it without looking at your phone.

**3. Start your Best Takes folder (3 minutes a day, for 7 days).** Record the same 30-second passage 3 times. Keep the best one, name it with the date, and write one thing that went well.

**4. Speak to yourself before you sing (1 minute, before Sunday).** Use your own name: "Name, your heart is racing because this matters. Give them the first line clearly." Say it 3 times out loud, then sing.

**Your proof:** a list with the P lines crossed out, one song you can lead without your phone, and 7 dated Best Takes.
