"But it sounded fine to me."

You have probably said it after a rehearsal. Someone mentioned that the second chorus went flat, and you were sure it didn't. You were there. You heard yourself.

How can another person know your voice better than you do?

The uncomfortable answer is that, in the moment, they often can. The fix is to train the part of you that judges.

This post is built around the questions singers ask when that conversation comes up. I will take them one at a time.

## "Why did it sound fine to me?"

Because you were not hearing what the room heard.

Think about the first time you played back a WhatsApp voice note of yourself. Most people pull a face. "Is that how I sound?"

It is. The phone removed part of what you normally hear, and left the rest.

Timothy Hullar, an ear specialist at Washington University School of Medicine, explains it in Scientific American. Sound reaches your inner ear by 2 routes. One comes through the air and into your ear canal, like every other sound.

The other travels through the bones and tissues of your own head, and your head boosts the deeper, lower vibrations on the way. Everybody else hears only the air route. You hear both, so the voice in your head is fuller and deeper than the voice in the room.

That example is about tone, the colour of the voice. Pitch is a separate question. But it shows you something important.

**The singer is the one person in the building who never hears the song from the outside.**

And while you sing, your attention is busy with words, breath, the band and the congregation. Very little of it is left for listening.

## "Surely a trained singer can tell?"

Not as well as you would think.

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics in Germany, working with New York University, recorded 18 highly trained sopranos singing "Happy Birthday". Each singer then rated the pitch accuracy of her colleagues' recordings and of her own. Pitch accuracy means how close each note was to the note it was meant to be.

The researchers used each singer's ratings of others to check how fairly she rated herself. Most of them rated themselves higher than they deserved.

There was a second finding, and it is the one I hold on to. The more accurate the singer, the more accurately she judged herself. Good singing and good self-judgement went together.

The study has limits. 18 singers is a small group, all women, all sopranos, with one simple song and one skill. It says nothing directly about a gospel lead singer in Lagos or Kaduna. Still, the pattern deserves attention.

In the Handbook I wrote, "You know yourself; you are your best critic." I still agree with that line, with the condition I attached to it: if you have worked on yourself long and well enough. Nobody is born their own best critic. You become one.

That distance, between what you think you sang and what you sang, is **the listening gap**. Most singers have one. The real question is whether yours is growing or shrinking.

## "So should I stop trusting my own ear?"

No. That would be the wrong lesson, and the research does not support it.

A 2023 study from the University of Melbourne asked 996 Australian twins, most of them ordinary members of the public, how good they thought they were at singing. Then it measured their pitch accuracy with an online singing test.

Their self-ratings lined up with their real ability fairly strongly. A single question, "How much singing ability do you have?", predicted almost as well as the full 16-question survey. The study measured how closely the 2 moved together, rather than how far each person was off. But it tells you that most people have a rough sense of where they stand.

So your ear is useful, but rough. It can tell you "I can sing" or "I struggle". It is less reliable at telling you "that third line went flat".

Here is where I must be fair to the singers on the other side. The listening gap does not only make people think they are better than they are. It also makes some people think they are worse.

Peter Pfordresher and Steven Brown, music psychologists then working in Texas and in Canada, tested this in 2007. Of the 79 people they tested in the lab, 39 had said beforehand that they could not sing a tune back. Only 4 of those 39 turned out to be poor-pitch singers, meaning they missed the notes by more than a semitone on average. A semitone is the smallest step on a keyboard, from one key to the next.

In other words, about 9 in 10 of the people who believed they couldn't sing in tune, could. Even the poor-pitch singers could hear the difference between notes as well as the good singers. Their trouble was turning the note they heard into the right action in the voice.

American university students are not Nigerian choristers, so I won't push the numbers too far. But ask yourself: how many singers in your choir decided long ago that they "can't hold a tune", without anyone ever checking?

## "How do I close the gap?"

Picture a good tailor in Aba. You walk in, and before you say a word he can guess your size by looking at you. He is usually close. He still reaches for the tape.

Why? Because the tape is how his eye got good. Thousands of guesses, each one checked against the tape, until the guess and the measurement started to agree. If he had stopped measuring after his first year, his eye would have stayed at first-year level.

This is how I would train your ear: **guess, then check**.

1. Record a short phrase on your phone, 4 to 8 bars. A bar is a small group of beats, usually 4, so this is about 10 to 25 seconds.
2. Before you play it back, give yourself a pitch score from 1 to 5. Name the one spot where you think you drifted.
3. Play it back with a check outside your own head. Open a free tuner app on a second phone while it plays, or ask someone with a good ear to listen with you. Don't skip this step.
4. Compare your guess with what you found. Write down both.

Step 3 matters more than it looks. In a 2016 study of 26 undergraduate musicians in the United States, listening back to their own recordings did not, on its own, make their self-judgements significantly more accurate. Playback alone was not enough. They needed something to measure against.

A small study at the University of Sydney in 2006 points the same way. Singers of all levels who saw their pitch on a screen while they sang, the way a tuner app shows it, improved their pitch accuracy after one hour of training. Neither study is large. Together they suggest the tape needs to be in the room.

Paul put a version of this to the Galatians:

> "But let every man prove his own work, and then shall he have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another." (Galatians 6:4)

Prove his own work. In the old sense of the word, to prove something is to test it. Paul asks for a test.

Then you can rejoice in what you have done. You no longer need to measure yourself by another singer's applause or another singer's mistakes. Your confidence stands on evidence you checked.

> **A trained ear is a guessing ear that kept getting checked.**

## "Who else should I listen to?"

A recording and a tuner will tell you about pitch. They will not tell you whether the congregation could follow you, or whether your face matched the song. For that you need people.

In the Handbook I wrote that there is always that sincere person around who is not a sycophant: someone observant, with some knowledge of music, who sees what most people miss and is ready to say it. Find that person. Then make it easy for them to speak. A singer who argues with every comment soon stops hearing any.

One caution. Not every comment is a fact. A choir member who says "you should sing it higher" may be telling you what they like. Use the tuner to settle the pitch questions, and keep that honest listener for the things a machine can't hear.

## Your next 5 days

Pick the hardest line in the first song you will lead next Sunday. Run "guess, then check" on it once a day for 5 days: guess your score, check it against the tuner or a listener, write both numbers down.

On the 5th day, look at your 2 columns. Did your guesses get closer to what the check showed?

Then send one voice note to your honest listener and answer this question in it: "Which line do I always think I sing well, but probably don't?"

Their answer will show you where your listening gap is widest. Start there.
