Almost every Sunday, somewhere between the benediction and the car park, a singer takes a colleague by the arm and asks the same question in a low voice: "Did you hear it?" They mean the note. It is the one in the second verse that came out thin, or a little flat, or cracked in the middle like a plastic chair that has finally given up.

In my experience, the honest answer, more often than not, is "Hear what?" And when someone does say "Yes, I noticed", it is very often because of what came after the note: the wince, the little shake of the head, or the eyes flying to the keyboardist as if to say, "It wasn't me, it was the mic."

That is the idea this whole post stands on, and I call it **the second mistake**. The first mistake is the note. The second mistake is announcing it.

You can't always prevent the first one, because live music is live and voices are human. I have led over 1,000 live sessions, and I still can't promise that every note in the next one will behave. The second mistake, though, is usually a choice, and it is often the one people remember.

So let me take the questions singers ask about this, one at a time.

## Did everybody really hear it?

Far fewer people heard it than you think. Back in 1996 a researcher called Bruno Repp had pianists play pieces they knew well, then played the recordings to listeners, including trained musicians, and many of the wrong notes the pianists really played simply went unnoticed. That was piano and not voice, but it shows that listeners miss more than we fear.

Psychologists also have a name for the feeling that the whole room is watching your every slip: the spotlight effect. In a well-known Cornell study in 2000, students who wore an embarrassing T-shirt into a room guessed that about half the people there had noticed it, when only about a quarter had. Hold that one loosely, because it was American students and a T-shirt, but it fits: we overestimate how closely people track our mistakes.

And here is one that surprised me. A researcher at University College London, Chia-Jung Tsay, played people short clips from top classical music competitions in 2013 and asked them to pick the winners, and they picked the real winners more often from silent video than from clips with sound. It was about choosing winners and not about mistakes, but it suggests your face carries more of the verdict than we think.

Put all that together. Most of the congregation probably didn't hear the note clearly, and the people who did are watching your face as much as they are listening.

## So what do I do with my face?

You keep it on the song. Every song you lead carries a message, whether that is joy in a praise song or quiet surrender in a slow worship song. When a note goes wrong, your only job is to go back to the face that carries that message, the same way you would carry on talking to a friend after you mispronounced a word, without stopping to explain the dictionary to them.

In practice that comes down to a few small habits. Your eyes stay where they were, and they don't dart to the band to find someone to blame. Your free hand stays away from your throat, because touching your throat after a crack shows the whole church exactly where to look. And you don't giggle it off from the stage, unless the moment is already light and the church is laughing with you, because a nervous giggle is really just a wince that has dressed up in agbada.

Here is where I must be careful, though. I am not asking you to fix a big smile on your face and freeze it there. A frozen smile is its own kind of tension, because it tightens the jaw and the tongue, and a tight jaw makes the next note harder to sing.

If you start watching your own face like a security man at the gate, you will become mechanical, and then the watching becomes the problem. You are not hiding anything from the congregation. You are simply refusing to make your mistake the main event.

## Should I stop and start again?

In most cases you shouldn't, and here is why. Music moves on time, and from where I stand leading, a congregation follows the rhythm much more closely than the exact pitch. If you stop, the band either stops with you or carries on without you; either way, the room now knows.

So keep the rhythm going and aim for the next **downbeat**, which is the strong first beat of a bar, the one you would naturally clap or step on. If the line is already broken, let the rest of it go, take a quick breath, and come in cleanly at the start of the next **phrase**, which is a musical sentence, about one line of the lyric long.

Think of a danfo driver who has missed his turning on the expressway. He doesn't reverse into the traffic to fix it. He takes the next turning, and the passengers hardly notice.

Now, in fairness, there are times when stopping is the right call. If the song started in a key you simply cannot sing, or the band and the singers have fallen apart in different places, a short, calm reset is far better than 4 minutes of struggle. Give the keyboardist a small hand signal, say something like "Let's sing that again together", and start, and the congregation will follow you into it. What I am warning you against is the panic stop after one bad note in a song that was otherwise going well.

## What if it cracks again on the next line?

It helps to know what a crack is.

A voice crack is usually a sudden, unplanned jump between **registers**, which are the different gears of your voice. You might jump from the heavier chest voice into the lighter head voice without meaning to. It tends to happen because you pushed more air or more weight into a note than it could carry. So the worst thing you can do on the next line is push harder to prove a point.

Do the opposite instead. On the next attempt, make the **onset**, the very start of the note, gentle, and sing a little lighter.

If the note still isn't there, choose a safer one: sing the line an octave lower, or take a lower harmony note you know well. You can settle your quarrel with that high note at home on Tuesday, when nobody is watching. If the high notes keep cracking, I explained why pushing makes them worse in [Stop shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/).

Paul has a line I find useful in that moment, even though he was writing about far bigger things than a cracked note:

> "...this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before" (Philippians 3:13)

Look at the order he puts it in. Forgetting comes first, and then reaching. You can't reach for the next line while your hands are still full of the last one.

> **The note lasted 1 second. Don't let your face make it last the whole service.**

## What about when it's someone on my team?

If you lead, your face is doing double duty. When a backing singer comes in early, the whole team looks at you, and if you glare, you have told the congregation and that singer that it was a disaster.

In The Lead Singer's Handbook I wrote that people do enough beating themselves up when they perform below expectations, so sometimes you simply let the error slide, "because sometimes it's just not our day." I still stand by that. If it really matters, raise it calmly at the next rehearsal rather than with your eyes in the middle of worship.

And if you sing backing vocals and the lead cracks, please don't turn your head to look at them. Keep singing your part, and you will have covered for your lead without anybody knowing.

## How do I practise for a mistake?

The 2 seconds after a mistake can be trained, but most of us never train them. At home we practise like this: we sing, we miss a note, we stop, we sing the line again, and we stop again. That is fine for fixing notes, but your body slowly learns that a mistake means "stop".

So add one new kind of practice to your week. Set your phone on a table or a stack of books, start a video recording, and do a full **run-through**, which means singing the whole song from the first line to the last **cut-off**, the point where everyone stops together, as if the congregation were in front of you, without stopping for anything. When a note goes wrong, you keep your face on the song and come in on the next phrase, exactly as you would on Sunday.

Then watch the video back, and this is how you measure progress. Count every visible reaction to a mistake, from a wince or a head shake to a glance at the band, and that number is your **wince count**.

Write it down, then do another run-through next week and again in 4 weeks, and compare. You want the wince count going down even while the odd mistake is still there. If the voices in your head get loud while you watch yourself back, I wrote about them in [The panel of judges in your head](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/panel-of-judges-in-your-head/).

One honest exception: if your voice cracks often, stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or singing starts to hurt, that is not a face problem. Please see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist.

Your challenge for this week is to pick the song you are most likely to lead this Sunday and record one full run-through on video before Saturday, then write down your wince count. On Sunday, if a note goes wrong, give yourself one job only, which is to keep your face on the song and come in on the next phrase. Then on Monday, ask yourself honestly: did anybody know, apart from me?
