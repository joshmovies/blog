I once went with my team to an all-night concert after a series of rugged preparations. We had worked on a medley of several songs, and we were full and brimming with confidence, ready to set the stage on fire for Christ.

When we arrived, we found that the hosts had prepared beyond our expectation. The sound was in top shape, and the musical equipment on ground was the very latest, which my instrumentalists were not used to at the time. But they didn't speak out.

We started the first song, and what they played out of those instruments was an absolute mess. And right there, in front of everybody, I gave my guys a look. It wasn't a small look either. It was a full, victimising look, the kind that says, "What is this you people are doing to me?"

I'll tell you how that night ended later. For now, I want you to see what I did, because in this post I am the bad example.

The people in that hall didn't know the equipment was new to my guys, and many of them may not have known anything was wrong at all, until they saw my face. My look didn't fix a single note. All it did was tell the whole room that my team had failed, and it gave them up.

That's the idea this post stands on, and I call it **the second mistake**. The first mistake is the wrong note, whether it comes from you or from the band. The second mistake is announcing it with your face.

In the Handbook I even wrote that you should "avoid wearing looks that express your dissatisfaction". And I'll be honest with you: to this day, I am still guilty of it, even though I try. So this post is for me as much as it is for you.

## Did they really hear it?

Far fewer people hear our mistakes than we think. Back in 1996, a researcher called Bruno Repp played recordings of pianists to listeners, including trained musicians, and many of the wrong notes the pianists really played simply went unnoticed. That was piano and not a church band, so hold it loosely.

What the people in that hall could read was my face. People take their cue from the person at the front, so if you carry on as though the song is going well, most of them will carry on with you.

## So what do I do with my face?

You keep it on the song. Every song you lead carries a message, joy in a praise song or quiet surrender in a worship song. When a note goes wrong, yours or the band's, your only job is to go back to the face that carries that message, the same way you would keep talking to a friend after mispronouncing a word.

In practice, your eyes stay where they were, and they don't dart to the band to find someone to blame. Your free hand stays away from your throat, because touching your throat shows the whole church exactly where to look. And you don't giggle it off from the stage unless the moment is already light, because a nervous giggle is really just a wince that has dressed up in agbada.

Here is where I must be careful, though. I am not asking you to fix a big smile on your face and freeze it there. A frozen smile is its own kind of tension, because it tightens the jaw and the tongue, and a tight jaw makes the next note harder to sing. If you start watching your own face like a security man at the gate, you will become mechanical, and then the watching becomes the problem.

## It gets worse than a look

Whether the wrong note is yours or the band's, the face is where it shows, and my look was the mild version of something much worse. I have heard of a music director who threw objects at his musicians for missing a beat. I have seen lead singers walk off stage in the middle of a ministration, and I know of some who have gone as far as slapping their backing vocalists for errors.

Every one of those comes from the same place as my look. Something went wrong, the leader felt embarrassed in front of the people, and the embarrassment came out on the team. So the congregation now has a bigger show to watch than the wrong note ever was.

If you lead, this part is for you. In fairness to us leaders, the pressure is real. You prepared, you are the one at the front, and when the sound falls apart it feels like your own name is being spoiled. Correction has its place too, but that place is the next rehearsal, in private and with a calm voice, and never the stage with your eyes or your hands.

## Keep going, or change the plan

In most cases you shouldn't stop. Music moves on time, and from where I stand leading, a congregation follows the rhythm much more closely than the exact pitch.

So keep the rhythm going and aim for the next **downbeat**, which is the strong first beat of a bar, the one you would naturally clap or step on. If the line is already broken, let the rest of it go, take a quick breath, and come in cleanly at the start of the next **phrase**, which is a musical sentence, about one line of the lyric long. Think of a danfo driver who has missed his turning on the expressway. He doesn't reverse into the traffic to fix it, he simply takes the next turning, and the passengers hardly notice.

But sometimes the problem is bigger than one note, and that brings me back to the concert. After that look, I knew we needed God's wisdom, and the depth of our preparation, to save the night. I spoke a few words to bring back the attention and confidence of the people, and then I changed the line-up. We left the medley we had rehearsed and switched to songs and a style my guys could carry on equipment they didn't know yet, and that ministration turned out to be our best outing ever.

The look only made things worse. What saved the night was the switch, and the switch was only possible because we had prepared more than one plan. When the band clearly can't carry what you planned, a calm change of direction is leadership: a small signal to the keyboardist, a few words to the people, and a new song.

## When your voice breaks on a note

Let me clear up a word we use differently. Where I come from, when we say a singer's voice has "cracked", we usually mean the voice is stressed out and hoarse, whether from illness, too much singing or shouting, a sore throat, a bad singing style, or a key that doesn't suit you. Voice teachers use the word for something narrower. In standard voice teaching, a crack is more precisely called a **voice break**. It is a sudden, unplanned flip between **registers**, which are the different gears of your voice.

Both are real, but they are different problems. A voice that stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or hurts when you sing, needs a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist. In this post I mean the sudden break, for example when a note jumps from your heavier chest voice into your lighter head voice without your permission.

A break tends to happen because you pushed more air or more weight into a note than it could carry. So on the next line, don't push harder to prove a point. Make the **onset**, the very start of the note, gentle, and sing a little lighter, and if the note still isn't there, sing the line an octave lower and settle your quarrel with that high note at home on Tuesday. If your high notes keep breaking, I explained why pushing makes them worse in [Stop shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/).

Paul has a line I find useful here, even though he was writing about far bigger things than a wrong note:

> "...this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before" (Philippians 3:13)

Forgetting comes first, and then reaching. You can't reach for the next line while your hands are still full of the last one.

> **The note lasted 1 second. Don't let your face make it last the whole service.**

## Practise finishing the song

You can't rehearse a surprise exactly, but you can practise not stopping. Most of us practise by stopping every time we miss a note, and that slowly teaches the body that a mistake means "stop".

So once a day this week, sing one song you lead along with its recording, from the first line to the last, and don't stop for anything. If you miss a note or forget a word, keep singing and come in on the next line, with your face on the song, the way you would on Sunday.

Then write a **plan B** beside that song: one easier option, such as a lower key or another song in the same mood that your band already plays well. That is the part that saved my night, and it is the difference between a look and a switch.

Your proof is simple. Each day, tick whether you got to the end without stopping, and by next week those ticks should be coming easily. Then on Sunday, if something goes wrong, from you or from the band, give your face one job, which is to stay on the song, and let your plan B do the rest.
