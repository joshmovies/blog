Let me start with a definition, because the rest of this post depends on it.

Leading a song is getting other people to sing it with you. That is the whole job. If you sang beautifully and the congregation stood there watching you, you performed. You did not lead.

I grew up around church music in Warri. Back then, a "good singer" was the one with the biggest voice and the longest runs. People clapped for that singer. But clapping for a singer is not the same as singing with one.

## Your congregation is not your audience

An audience watches. A choir sings. When you stand in front of a church, you are not facing an audience. You are facing a choir of 50, 200 or 2,000 untrained voices who need you to carry them.

Think of a host who cooks a big pot of jollof for a party, then sits down and eats it alone while the guests watch. The food can be excellent. The party has still failed.

This is not only my opinion. In 2025, the Worship Leader Research team surveyed about 3,400 worship leaders in North America. About 98% of them said the way they judge a new song is by watching how the congregation responds to it. Not by how well the band played it. Not by how good the lead singer sounded. By whether the people sang.

That survey is from North America, and our churches are different. But the principle travels. The measure of your leading is the sound coming from the pews, not the sound coming from your microphone.

## The participation test

I call it the participation test. After every service you lead, ask yourself one question:

**Did the people sing more when I came in, or less?**

If they sang less, something in your leading pushed them out. Usually it is one of 3 things.

### 1. You chose a key for yourself, not for them

Most people in your church are not trained singers. Their comfortable range is small. If you start a song in a key that shows off your top notes, the lower voices drop out first. Then the rest follow. Then you are singing alone.

Choose the key for the people. Your solo moments can come later.

### 2. Your words are not clear

A congregation follows your words before it follows your melody. If they cannot hear what you are singing, they cannot join you. A June 2026 training for church musicians at Wesley Methodist Church made this point directly: clear diction on congregational songs matters, so people can follow the words.

Swallowed consonants and runs on every line make the song hard to follow. Sing the words so a visitor could catch them the first time.

### 3. Your team is not prepared

The same Wesley training used a role play of a badly prepared choir. Wrong notes, phones out, no communication between the conductor and the pianist. You can guess what happened to the congregation. They went flat and quiet.

Permit me to speak a bit bluntly. When your team is not prepared, the congregation feels it before they can explain it. They stop trusting the song, so they stop singing it.

## Now let me establish balance

I am not saying you should never sing a solo or never stretch your voice. There are moments in a service for a special number, and there are songs where the congregation listens while you minister. That is valid.

The problem is when every song becomes that moment. Know which song is for them and which song is for you, and do not confuse the two.

## Voice and leading are 2 skills

I have trained singers for about 15 years. A strong voice is one skill. Leading people is another. Leading means you listen to the room while you sing.

If you never had formal training, that is not an insult. The same 2025 survey found that most worship leaders have no worship-related degree either. It just means nobody taught us that leading and performing are 2 different skills. You can learn the first one.

> Your voice is the tool. The people singing is the result.

## What to do this Sunday

Start doing:
- Pick 1 song you lead regularly and lower the key by 1 or 2 steps. Watch who joins in.
- Before the service, read the lyrics aloud once, slowly, and make every consonant clear.

Stop doing:
- Adding runs to the first verse. Let the people learn the melody first.

Test after the service:
- Ask 1 person in the congregation, not on the team, "Could you sing along today?" Listen to the answer without defending yourself.

Then run the participation test again next week and compare.
