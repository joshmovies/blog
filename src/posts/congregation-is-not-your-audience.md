Let me start with a definition, because everything in this post stands on it.

Leading a song is getting other people to sing it with you. That is the whole assignment. If you sang beautifully and the congregation stood there watching you, you performed. You did not lead.

Do we understand this? Because most of us were trained for the opposite.

## What we were taught a good singer is

I grew up in Warri, and back then the majority's idea of a good singer was built from a set of "facts". You must sing powerfully. You must hit the high notes. The accepted style was loud, heavy and forceful, and the song was not complete until it reached that shouted climax.

Singers were built, trained and encouraged after that picture. So we learned to stand in front of the church and show what our voices could do. People clapped for it. But clapping for a singer is not the same as singing with one.

Those "facts" were preferences. They told you what impressed people. They never told you what helps people sing.

## Your congregation is not your audience

An audience watches. A choir sings. When you stand in front of a church, you are not facing an audience. You are facing a choir of 50, 200 or 2,000 voices, most of them untrained, waiting for you to carry them.

Think of a tour guide in a crowded market. If he walks at his own pace, looking back only to admire his own steps, he will reach the other end alone and very fast. He will also have lost everybody he was paid to guide. A good guide checks behind him, slows down at the busy junction and waits at the corner until the last person catches up.

That is the job on Sunday. The question to keep asking is "Who is still with me?"

This is not only my opinion. In 2025 the Worship Leader Research team surveyed about 3,400 worship leaders in North America. About 98% of them said they judge a new song by watching how the congregation engages with it. Not by how well the band played it. Not by how good the lead singer sounded. By whether the people sang.

That survey is from North America, and our churches are different. But the principle travels, because Scripture said it long before any survey did:

> "O magnify the Lord with me, and let us exalt his name together." (Psalm 34:3)

With me. Together. The psalmist was not performing for an audience. He was gathering a choir.

## The participation test

I call it **the participation test**. After every service you lead, ask yourself one question:

**Did the people sing more when I came in, or less?**

If they sang less, something in your leading pushed them out. It usually comes down to one of 3 things: the key, the words or the team.

### 1. You chose the key for your voice, not for theirs

Most people in your church are not trained singers. Their comfortable range is small. Start a song in the key that shows off your top notes, and the lower voices drop out first. The rest follow one by one. By the second chorus you are singing alone with a microphone, and you will wonder why the atmosphere is dry.

Think of a host who cooks a big pot of jollof for a party. The food is excellent. Then he sits down and eats it alone while the guests watch. The food did not fail. The party did.

In the Handbook I wrote that if you must change key, the transition phase is the place to do it, before the peak, because it can be done there without causing much damage. The same thinking applies from the first song. Choose the key for the people. Your solo moments can come later.

### 2. Your words are not clear

A congregation follows your words before it follows your melody. If they cannot hear what you are singing, they cannot join you.

Look at how Ezra's team handled the public reading of the law:

> "So they read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading." (Nehemiah 8:8)

Distinctly. Gave the sense. Caused them to understand. That is diction, phrasing and clarity, written down thousands of years before anybody bought a microphone.

A June 2026 training for church musicians at Wesley Methodist Church made the same point in modern terms: clear diction on congregational songs matters, so people can follow the words. Swallowed consonants and runs on every line turn a song into a puzzle. A visitor should be able to catch the words the first time you sing them.

### 3. Your team is not prepared

The same Wesley training used a role play of a badly prepared choir. Wrong notes, phones out, no communication between the conductor and the pianist. You can guess what happened. The congregation went flat and quiet.

Permit me to speak a bit bluntly. When your team is not prepared, the congregation feels it before they can explain it. Picture a danfo driver who keeps braking and changing lanes without warning. The passengers hold on to the seat in front and stop chatting. Nobody relaxes in a bus they don't trust. People stop trusting the song, so they stop singing it.

## Expression is how you truly lead

There is a line from the Handbook I still stand on: **you cannot take anyone where you have not first been.**

If you are singing about the goodness of God, let your face confirm that He has been good to you. If you are giving thanks, carry a mood of thanksgiving. A leader who stands like an immovable mountain, then blames the congregation for not responding, has missed the point. What leads is genuine, heart-originated expression, backed by a real understanding of the song you are singing.

So the participation test is not only technical. It is also a heart check. Did I sing this song as someone who has been where I'm asking them to go?

## Now let me establish balance

I am not saying you should never sing a solo or stretch your voice. There are moments in a service for a special number. There are songs where the congregation listens while you minister, and the Spirit uses that too.

The problem is when every song becomes that moment. Know which song is for them and which song is for you, and don't confuse the two.

## Voice and leading are 2 skills

I have led music since 2009, led over 1,000 live sessions and trained over 500 vocalists. A strong voice is one skill. Leading people is another. Leading means you listen to the room while you sing.

If you never had formal training, that is not an insult. The same 2025 survey found that most worship leaders have no worship-related degree either. It just means nobody taught us that leading and performing are 2 different skills. The first one can be learned, and you can start this week.

> **Your voice is the tool. The people singing is the result.**

## Do it now

**1. Take the participation test (5 minutes, after Sunday).** Write down the songs you led. Next to each, mark whether the people sang more, the same or less. Circle the song where they sang least.

**2. Run the key check (10 minutes, before your next rehearsal).** Take that circled song and sing it 2 steps lower. Then ask 1 person who is not on the music team to sing it with you in both keys. Ask which one felt comfortable. Write down the answer, not your preference.

**3. Read it distinctly (3 minutes, before the service).** Read the lyrics of your first 3 songs out loud, slowly, as if you were Ezra's team reading to the people. Make every consonant clear. Then sing them the same way.

**4. Collect one honest answer (1 minute, after the service).** Ask 1 person in the congregation, "Could you sing along today?" Listen without defending yourself.

**Your proof:** 2 sets of marks, this Sunday's and next Sunday's, side by side. If the circled song moves from "less" to "more", you have just learned to lead.
