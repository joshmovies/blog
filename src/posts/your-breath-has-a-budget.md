Salary comes in on the 25th. By the 10th of the next month, some people are already asking a colleague for "something small till month end".

Nobody plans to run out. They just spent the way the money felt on the first day, which was plenty. The last 2 weeks paid for the first one.

Singers do the same thing with air.

Watch a choir on a long line. The first few words come out big and warm, like a man who has just been paid. By the last word, half the tenors have gone quiet and the sopranos are gasping in the middle of a word. They spent the breath the way it felt on the first note.

Your breath has a budget, and most of us are bad with money.

You will hear teachers call it "breath support". Strip away the mystery and support is simply your ability to decide how fast the air leaves you.

Most singers think their problem is how much air they can take in. So they gulp. They lift their shoulders. They fill up like a balloon at a children's party. Then they spend half of it on the first 2 words of the song.

The size of the breath matters far less than how fast you spend it. Ordinary lungs with control will usually outlast big lungs that pour everything into the first word.

In the Handbook I wrote that "the majority of the problems singers have can be found in their inability to tame their breathing process". I still believe it. Trace a flat ending or a shaky long note back, and you will often find a phrase that ran out of money before the month did.

> **A breath you did not plan is a breath you will borrow, usually from the middle of a word.**

So here is how to manage the money, plus one warning that may matter most.

## Let the breath in. Don't manufacture it

Something we don't say enough: breathing in is natural. You do it more than 20,000 times a day without a single lesson ([Canadian Lung Association](https://www.lung.ca/lung-health/lung-info/breathing)). When you breathe in, your diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, tightens and flattens on its own, and the air comes in to fill the space. Your body already knows this job.

So the work is mostly about getting out of the way.

The most common thing that gets in the way is the "big breath" habit. Ask a singer to take a deep breath and watch the shoulders shoot up like someone has just called their name in a meeting. That is high breathing. It feels like a lot of air, but the neck and shoulders tense, and a tight neck makes a tight sound.

A relaxed breath looks quieter. The shoulders stay where they are. The lower ribs widen a little, and you feel the belly, waist and lower back loosen and move outwards.

If you want to check yourself, rest one hand on your belly and one on the side of your waist and breathe in through your nose. If your hands move gently and your shoulders stay down, you are fine. Don't stand there for 20 minutes studying it.

Don't overdo it either. Voice researcher Sally Collyer reports that singers usually start a phrase at about 70 to 80% of the most air they can take in; above that, the lungs push back like a stretched rubber band ([Collyer, NATS ICVT handout](https://www.nats.org/_Library/ICVT7/ICVT7_09_Collyer_Breathing_science_handout.pdf)). If your breath feels like a suitcase you had to sit on to close, you took too much. And between lines, a quick, quiet breath through the mouth is enough. If the sound engineer can hear your breath louder than your words, it was too big, too high or too late.

## Spend it slowly

This is where the budget lives.

Higher notes need a bit more breath pressure, and that pressure also affects pitch. Johan Sundberg, a leading voice scientist, showed that this is why singers need fine breath control to stay in tune ([Sundberg, Journal of Singing, 1993](https://www.nats.org/_Library/Kennedy_JOS_Files_2013/JOS-049-3-1993-004.pdf)). So when your air is uneven, your pitch goes uneven. A singer who goes flat at the end of every long phrase may not have a hearing problem at all. They may have a spending problem.

Two habits help:

- **Don't collapse.** After the breath, let the belly come back in gradually, but don't let the chest cave in on the first word. When the chest drops, the air rushes out with it.
- **Give high notes a little more, not a blast.** Press the way you would press a car's accelerator on a hill: steady, not stamping. A sudden shove of air makes the note harsh and leaves you broke for the next line. I wrote about this in [Stop shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/).

## The warning: don't become a breathing machine

Now let me be careful, because this is where good singers get into trouble.

Give breathing attention in practice, then leave it alone. Some singers learn about breathing and suddenly every song becomes a science project. They are counting, checking their belly, tightening muscles, thinking about the diaphragm in the middle of the chorus. The voice goes stiff. The face goes serious. The congregation can see the homework.

Now the breathing is the problem.

Teachers have described "support" in so many different ways that a singer told "more support!" every week can end up rigid and tense ([Manternach, Journal of Singing, 2024](https://nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS-081-2-2024-205.pdf)). Support should feel firm and steady, never forced. If your neck, jaw or shoulders tighten, you have started squeezing.

So practise breath in your practice time. Then, when you minister, forget about it and sing. The habit you built in the week is what shows up on Sunday. You don't drive your car by thinking about the engine.

And breath is not the whole story. If your tone is breathy, air may be leaking because the vocal folds are not closing well, even when your breathing is fine. That is a different conversation, for another day.

## Plan where you breathe

Choirs and worship teams win or lose a song right here.

Most singers breathe when they run out. A singer with a budget breathes where the words allow.

Before Sunday, take the longest line in your set and agree where everyone breathes. Choose places where the meaning pauses: a comma, the end of a thought. Not in the middle of a word, and please don't split "Your" from "name". Put a small tick on the lyric sheet so the team breathes together.

On a long held note, don't let the whole choir breathe at the same time and leave a hole in the sound. Let each singer sneak a quick breath at a different moment and come back in softly. The congregation hears one unbroken sound. Nobody needs to know you were all taking turns.

If you lead, you have 2 extra challenges.

First, you talk. You exhort, pray and give instructions, then sing again straight away. Take a quiet breath before the next line, even if it costs you a moment. A short pause hardly registers. A gasp into the microphone does.

Second, nerves. Anxiety makes breathing fast and high. When you feel it, slow down your next 3 breaths and let them drop. I wrote more about that pressure in [The panel of judges in your head](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/panel-of-judges-in-your-head/).

## "Every thing that hath breath"

> "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD." (Psalm 150:6)

The last psalm lists trumpets, harps, timbrels, strings, organs and cymbals, then ends on the one instrument everyone in the building already owns.

The only qualification it names is breath. Not range, not tone. And that breath is given to be spent on praise. If breath is the gift, then how we spend it is stewardship. A budget is just good stewardship with a calculator.

## Try it this week

Keep it simple.

At your next rehearsal, take the longest line of your first song for Sunday. Sing it once on a gentle "sss", then on "oo", then with the words. Notice the word where the air runs out. Take the breath earlier, before that word, mark it, and sing it again.

Then record the team singing that line on your phone. Do the same in 4 weeks. Listen to the last word. If it arrives whole, with no gasp before it, your budget is working.

If a singer's voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or singing hurts, they should see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist.

And on Sunday, don't think about any of this. Just sing, and spend your breath on purpose.
