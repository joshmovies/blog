You know harmattan has arrived when Vaseline becomes a member of the family. Lips crack, heels crack, the furniture wears a fine brown coat by noon, and somebody in the house wakes up with a nosebleed. In West Africa the season runs roughly from the end of November to the middle of March, and on the worst days the air can drop below 15% humidity ([Harmattan, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmattan)). It bites hardest in the north, in places like Abuja, Jos and Kano, but Lagos feels it too.

Then you stand up on Sunday morning to sing, and your voice sounds like it slept outside.

Most singers answer this with one tool: a bottle of water. Good. Keep it. But that bottle only fixes half the problem, and I want to show you the other half.

I call it **the two waters**. Your voice needs water from the inside and water on the outside, and harmattan steals both.

The inside water is what you drink. It reaches your whole body, including the tissue of your vocal folds, the two small bands in your throat that vibrate to make sound. Voice scientists call this systemic hydration. The outside water is the thin, wet layer that coats the surface of the folds. They call this surface hydration, and it depends on the air you breathe ([Sivasankar and Leydon, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2925668/)). Harmattan air has almost nothing to give.

Here is the part many singers miss. When you swallow, your airway closes so the water goes down to your stomach. The water you swallow does not wash over your vocal folds on its way down. So you can drink 2 litres and still breathe dry dust across your folds all morning.

The easiest way to see both waters at work is to walk through one harmattan Sunday, from Saturday night to Sunday afternoon.

## Saturday, 10pm: the fan and the open mouth

Your Sunday voice is decided the night before.

Picture it. The fan is on full, or the AC is blowing, and you sleep with your mouth open. For 7 hours, dry air passes straight over your folds with nothing to warm it or wet it. You wake up and your throat feels like garri without water.

Your nose was made for this job. As air passes through it, the nose warms it and adds moisture before it reaches your throat. Breathing through the mouth skips that step.

A small lab study caught this in action. People breathed through the mouth for 15 minutes. Afterwards, their voices needed more pressure to start sounding, and speaking felt like more effort. Nasal breathing did not do that ([Sivasankar and Fisher, 2003](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2003/110))).

Voice scientists call that starting pressure phonation threshold pressure: how hard your breath must push before the folds begin to vibrate. Dry folds need a harder push. It was 15 minutes, not a whole harmattan night. Still, the lesson is plain: dry air in through the mouth makes the same song cost you more.

So on Saturday night, angle the fan away from your face and drink a glass of water before bed. If your room is very dry, a humidifier is worth having. A bowl of water in the corner is a nice gesture, but don't expect much from it.

## Sunday, 6am: the croak

You open your mouth to greet your mother, and what comes out sounds like a door that needs oil.

The natural response is to clear your throat. Hard. Several times. "Hm-HMM!"

Please don't. A forceful throat clear slaps your vocal folds together, and when they are already dry, that is the last thing they need. Voice clinics give a simple swap: sip water or swallow instead ([Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/-/media/entaa-care/documents/vocal-hygiene/vocal-hygiene-for-singers.pdf)). A gentle hum often shifts it too.

This is also the time for outside water. A simple steam works. The British Association for Performing Arts Medicine (BAPAM) advises pouring boiled water into a heatproof bowl and letting it cool for about 10 minutes first. Breathe the steam for 5 to 10 minutes, with no added oils.

Then rest your voice for 20 to 30 minutes before you sing. Never breathe steam from a kettle spout. If you have asthma, a heart condition or epilepsy, talk to a doctor first ([BAPAM](https://www.bapam.org.uk/saline-nebulisation-and-steam-inhalation/)). That rest time matters, so steam at 6am, not at 8:55 in the church toilet.

Some singers use a nebuliser with 0.9% saline, a fine salty mist breathed in through the mouth. In one small study, young men, singers and non-singers, breathed very dry air for 30 minutes, then felt less effort and less dryness after a saline mist ([Tanner and others, 2016](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/7251)). BAPAM says it needs no rest time afterwards. If you want one, buy it after a chat with your doctor or an ENT specialist, not because a blog said so.

Two more harmattan traps. If your nose is blocked or crusted dry, the nose plan falls apart, so ask a pharmacist about a saline nasal spray or rinse. And many catarrh tablets people reach for in this season, especially antihistamines, dry the throat further. If you need them, drink more, and ask a pharmacist or doctor before taking them on a singing morning.

Then drink. Not a whole bottle in one go. A glass now, and keep sipping through the morning.

## 7:30am: the dusty road to church

Okada, keke, or the window down in a car. Whichever way you travel, dust is in the air and the wind is in your face.

This is the most wasted 30 minutes of a singer's Sunday. Many of us spend the ride gisting at full volume, mouth open, over the engine noise. That is mouth breathing and loud talking at the same time, in the driest air of the week.

Keep your mouth closed and breathe through your nose. Save the gist for after service. If the dust is heavy, a scarf or face mask over your nose and mouth will hold back some of it.

David once wrote from the wilderness of Judah:

> "O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is." (Psalm 63:1)

He is talking about thirst for God, not about vocal folds, and that is the main point. But look at the order. He names the dry land honestly. Then he says "early". He does not wait until he is fainting before he looks for water. That is good counsel for your voice in harmattan. By the time your throat feels dry on the stage, you are late.

## 9am: the mic comes on

You are on the stand. The ceiling fans are spinning, the AC is cold, and the generator outside has been coughing smoke since 7. This hall is drier than your bedroom.

Three things matter here.

First, warm up a little longer than usual. A warm-up is a few minutes of gentle sounds before real singing, such as lip trills or humming, sliding from low to high. On a dry morning your folds are stiff, so give them 5 to 10 minutes instead of 2. Start soft, near your speaking pitch, in the comfortable middle of your range, before you climb.

Second, watch your onset, the way each note begins. When the voice feels dry, singers tend to force the first note out with a hard push from the throat. Aim for breath and sound arriving together, with no throat push and no puff of air first. If you keep catching a hard start, put a tiny "h" before the vowel a few times, then drop it.

Third, keep water where you can reach it during the service, and sip between songs, not in the middle of a phrase. Your catch breaths, the quick breaths you take between lines, will still go through the mouth. That's fine. A fast praise song leaves no time for nose breathing. I wrote about planning those breaths in [Your breath has a budget](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-breath-has-a-budget/). The nose rule is for the hours around the singing, not the singing itself.

If you lead, your speaking voice is on duty too. Every exhortation and every prayer in dry air is another withdrawal from the same account. Keep the talking shorter than you planned, and let the band hold a chord while you take a sip.

If you lead a team, put a small bottle of water at each singer's spot before service. It costs almost nothing, and a croaky alto on the second song will thank you.

## 1pm: after the service

Your voice has worked hard in dry air. Don't spend the afternoon shouting greetings across the car park.

Do a short cool-down: 2 minutes of gentle humming, sliding downward from the middle of your range. I described it in [Stop shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/). Then drink, and give your voice a quiet hour if you can.

## Water won't sing for you

Researchers have not proven that a healthy singer sounds better just by drinking extra water. What they have shown is that dry folds work harder, and that the air you breathe affects them ([Sivasankar and Leydon, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2925668/)). Good hydration removes a handicap. It will not fix your pitch or a song pitched in the wrong key.

Don't let this become an obsession either. Some singers start sipping after every line, clearing their throats between songs out of habit, and listening for dryness instead of singing. The body tightens, the face goes anxious, and the voice follows. Over-focusing on your throat becomes the problem. Do the work in the hours around the service. Sunday morning is for the song, not for checking your throat.

I serve as a lead vocalist in Abuja, where harmattan arrives with real dust. It humbles every voice in the building, the strong ones included.

And if your voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or singing hurts, or your voice goes, please see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist. Dryness can hide something else.

> **Drink for the inside. Breathe through your nose for the outside. Start on Saturday.**

## Your harmattan score

Take a note on your phone and call it "Harmattan voice".

After each Sunday service from now until March, write 2 numbers from 1 to 10. The first is how much effort singing took. The second is how dry your throat felt. Then record the first line of your opening song at 7am, before church, so you can hear the difference across the weeks.

This Saturday, do the two waters properly: fan away from your face, a glass of water before bed, a steam at 6am, a closed mouth on the road, a longer warm-up.

After 4 Sundays, look at your numbers. Which habit made the biggest difference for you? Keep that one, even when the rains come back.
