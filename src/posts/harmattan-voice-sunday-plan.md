The keyboardist plays the intro. The congregation is standing. You lift the microphone, open your mouth for the first line of a song you have sung 200 times, and out comes something that sounds like a goat clearing its throat.

You cough. You smile. You try again. By the second chorus it has come back, mostly. But for the rest of the service you are singing carefully, like a man walking on a floor that has just been mopped.

If it hasn't happened to you yet, give it a few weeks. Harmattan is here.

Most of us blame the morning. "My voice wasn't awake." I want to show you that the morning is innocent. That croak was arranged the night before, and you helped arrange it.

So let's rewind the tape.

## Rewind: Saturday night

It is 11pm. The fan is on full speed and pointed straight at your face, because the afternoon was hot. You fall asleep with your mouth slightly open. You know this because there is a small wet patch on the pillow in the morning, and we will not discuss it further.

For 7 hours, dry harmattan air has been blowing across your vocal folds, the two small bands in your throat that vibrate to make every note you sing. On the worst harmattan days in West Africa the air can drop below 15% humidity ([Harmattan, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmattan)). That is drier than a garri bag.

Your nose was built for exactly this. Air that comes in through the nose gets warmed and moistened on the way down. Air that comes in through the mouth arrives raw.

In one small American study, people breathed through the mouth for just 15 minutes, and afterwards their voices needed more push to start sounding, and talking felt like harder work. Breathing through the nose did not do that ([Sivasankar and Fisher, 2003](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2003/110))). 15 minutes. You gave yours 7 hours.

## Rewind further: the bottle you didn't drink

Saturday afternoon you were busy. Rehearsal, then an owambe, then traffic. Water? You had a Fanta and some small chops.

Then on Sunday at 8:50, in a panic, you drink half a bottle of water in the church corridor and wait for it to work.

Here is the part most singers don't know. When you swallow, your airway closes, so that water goes down to your stomach without ever touching your vocal folds. It still matters. Your body uses it to keep all its tissues moist, the folds included. But that takes time, not minutes. Drinking at 8:50 to fix a 9:00 croak is like charging your phone at the bus stop when the bus is already pulling up.

This is the idea I want you to keep. I call it **the two waters**.

The first water is the one you drink. It works from the inside, slowly, and it has to start the day before.

The second water is the moisture in the air you breathe. It works on the surface of your folds, right now. Harmattan steals this one, and no bottle can give it back.

## Rewind once more: the road to church

You took a keke. The wind was in your face, the dust was in the wind, and you spent the whole ride gisting with the sister beside you about the owambe. Loudly, because of the engine.

Mouth open, in dry dusty air, using your voice hard, 30 minutes before you sing. If I wanted to design a croak, I could not do a better job.

Now the morning looks different, doesn't it? The goat noise was not a mystery. It was a receipt.

## Sunday, take two

Let's play the same Sunday again, with 3 changes. Not 10. Three.

### 1. Start drinking on Saturday

Not a heroic 3 litres at once. A glass with every meal and one before bed, starting Saturday morning. Then keep sipping on Sunday through the morning, and keep a bottle where you can reach it during service. Sip between songs, not in the middle of a phrase, the line of music you sing on one breath.

A simple check: if your urine is pale, the first water is doing its job ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dehydration/)).

### 2. Close your mouth when you are not singing

Turn the fan so it blows across the room, not into your face. On the road, keep your mouth closed and breathe through your nose. Save the gist for after service; the sister will still be there. If the dust is heavy, a scarf over your nose and mouth helps.

To be clear, this is for the hours around the singing. When you sing, your catch breaths, the quick sips of air between lines, will still come through the mouth, and that's fine. A fast praise song leaves no time for nose breathing. I wrote about those breaths in [Your breath has a budget](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-breath-has-a-budget/).

### 3. Give your folds some steam, early

This is how you put the second water back. Pour boiled water into a bowl and let it cool for about 10 minutes. Then lean over it and breathe the steam gently for 5 to 10 minutes. Use a heatproof bowl on a table, never the kettle spout, and no oils. If you have asthma or a heart condition, ask your doctor first. The British Association for Performing Arts Medicine (BAPAM) advises resting your voice for 20 to 30 minutes afterwards before you sing ([BAPAM](https://www.bapam.org.uk/saline-nebulisation-and-steam-inhalation/)).

That rest time decides when you steam. 6am at home, yes. 8:55 in the church toilet, no.

Now play the tape. You did a slightly longer warm-up on the stand, a few minutes of soft humming near your speaking pitch. The keyboardist plays the intro. You lift the microphone. The first line comes out a little dry, maybe, but whole. You are singing the song, not tiptoeing around your own voice.

> **The croak you hear on Sunday was booked on Saturday.**

## Don't become a hydration inspector

Researchers have not proven that drinking extra water makes a healthy voice sound better on its own ([Sivasankar and Leydon, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2925668/)). It stops dryness from making everything harder, but it won't fix a shaky pitch. And don't carry this onto the stage. A singer who sips after every line and listens for trouble tightens up, and the voice follows. Do the work on Saturday. Once the song starts, your job is the song.

If your nose is blocked solid, or you take catarrh tablets (many dry the throat further), ask a pharmacist what is safe before a singing morning. If your voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or singing hurts, see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist.

David wrote one psalm in the wilderness of Judah, a dry and thirsty land:

> "O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is." (Psalm 63:1)

He is speaking about thirst for God, and that is the heart of it. But notice one small word. Early. He didn't wait until he was fainting before he went looking for water.

## This Saturday

Here is your one assignment. On Saturday night, before you sleep, do 2 things: drink a glass of water, and turn the fan away from your face.

On Sunday morning, before you leave the house, record the first line of your opening song on your phone. Do the same every Sunday until March. If you lead, note how many times you had to clear your throat during the service.

In 4 weeks, play the first recording and the latest one back to back. You will hear whether your Saturdays have changed.
