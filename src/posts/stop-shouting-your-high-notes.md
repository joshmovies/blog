Let me define a word first, because people use it loosely.

Your vocal range is the span of notes you can sing, from your lowest to your highest, with a healthy, steady sound. Notice the last part. A note you can only reach by shouting, straining or going red in the face sits outside your working range, even if you touched it once.

Every voice has natural limits. Training will never give you someone else's voice. What it does is help you use more of your own, with less effort.

## What we were taught about high notes

I grew up in Warri, and back then a good singer had to be loud, powerful and able to hit the high notes. The song was not finished until it reached that shouted climax. Many Nigerian churches still expect it.

So we learned one method for going higher: push. Open the mouth wider, tighten the neck, squeeze more air and force the note out. Sometimes it worked. Often it cracked. And by the end of a long Sunday, many of us were hoarse.

That was a preference about style, handed down as if it were technique. Loud and high are 2 different things, and force is the most expensive way to get either of them.

## The staircase rule

I call the better approach **the staircase rule**: you climb to high notes one step at a time, and you only stand on a step you can hold steadily.

Picture a keke going up a steep road in Jos. The driver who starts in a low gear and builds up gets to the top. The one who charges the hill in a high gear stalls halfway and rolls back. Same keke, same road. The difference is how the climb was managed.

Your voice works the same way. The singer who jumps from talking straight to the top of the song, at full force, is charging the hill in the wrong gear. So why do high notes strain? Here are the 3 reasons I see most.

### 1. You start cold and finish with force

In the Handbook I listed the benefits of a proper vocal exercise routine, and one of them was this: higher ranges are a benefit of appropriate vocal exercises. Most singers skip the exercises and go straight to the hardest song.

The exercise does not have to be complicated. A study at the University of Kansas split 14 professional singers into 2 groups. One group hummed through a drinking straw. The other did lip trills, the relaxed "brrr" sound with loose lips. Both did short sessions every day for 21 days. Both groups reported voices that felt easier and worked better, with no real difference between the 2 exercises. It is a small study, so treat it as a strong hint, not a law. But it means you don't need to buy anything. Your lips can do the job.

Start low, slide the trill up slowly, and stop at the top of what feels easy. Then do the same on an "oo" vowel before you add words. Each step tells your voice that the next one is safe.

In the Handbook I also warned that not all vocal exercises are appropriate, and not all appropriate exercises should be done by you. Keep it gentle. If anything hurts, stop.

### 2. You don't know where the note is

Many singers strain on high notes because they are reaching for a note they can't hear clearly in their head. They are guessing, and a guess usually comes with extra force, just to be sure.

This is where sol-fa helps. When you can name the note (do, re, mi, fa, so) and show it with your hand, you know exactly where you are going before you sing it. Studies that taught sol-fa with Curwen hand signs found better pitch accuracy than sol-fa without them. Most of those studies were with children, so for adults I present it as a helpful tool, not a proven fix. Here is a simple check I use: if you can't show the note with your hand, you probably can't hear it yet.

Scripture puts skill before volume:

> "Sing unto him a new song; play skilfully with a loud noise." (Psalm 33:3)

Skilfully. With a loud noise. The skill comes first in the sentence. The loud noise is welcome, but it rides on the skill. When we reverse that order, we get shouting.

### 3. You never let your voice come down

Permit me to speak a bit bluntly. Most of us treat our voice like a generator we run all night and never service.

A 2025 study in the Journal of Voice tested 30 adult choir singers. After just 15 minutes of choir singing, their measured voice quality actually improved, the way a warm-up would. But the singers themselves reported more tiredness and more vocal complaints. The researchers called for better warm-up and cool-down routines for choir singers. Again, it is one small study. Now think of our rehearsals: long, loud, in halls that echo, often with a generator running outside. Then we do it again on Sunday morning. A cool-down can be as simple as 2 minutes of gentle humming, sliding downward, before you pack up and go home.

Do we understand this? Range is built on recovery as much as on practice. A voice that is always tired will never feel free at the top.

## My own voice

I have a heavy, hoarse, dark-coloured timbre, and for years I struggled to accept it. I wanted the bright, high voices I heard around me. What helped was learning what my own voice could do well, and building upward from there, step by step, instead of copying someone else's top notes.

## Now let me establish balance

I am not telling you to sing quietly forever. Power is part of many styles we love, and some songs need that big moment. Strong, high singing is possible and healthy once the coordination is built.

I am also not promising that exercises will add a set number of notes to your range. Every voice has limits, and some notes may simply not be yours. That is fine. A singer who owns 10 steady notes will lead a room better than one who strains for 15.

And if you have hoarseness that lasts more than 2 to 3 weeks, pain when you sing or speak, or you lose your voice, please see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist. No blog post replaces that.

> **Skill first, then volume. Climb to your high notes. Never charge at them.**

## Do it now

**1. Find your steady steps (10 minutes, today).** With a piano or a keyboard app, sing "ah" from a comfortable middle note upward, one step at a time. Stop at the last note that stays steady without strain. Write it down. That is your working top for now.

**2. Lip trill before every practice (4 minutes, daily for 7 days).** Do relaxed lip trills, sliding gently from low to high and back down like a slow siren. Then sing your hardest line once.

**3. Sign it before you sing it (5 minutes, before your next rehearsal).** Take the highest phrase of a song you lead. Sing it slowly in sol-fa with hand signs before you sing the words.

**4. Cool down (2 minutes, after every rehearsal).** Hum gently and slide downward from the middle of your range to the bottom. Rate how your voice feels from 1 to 10 before and after.

**Your proof:** your working top note written on Day 1 and again on Day 7, plus 7 days of before-and-after voice ratings.
