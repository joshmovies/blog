Dear lead singer,

I watched you on Sunday. Well, maybe not you in particular, but someone just like you, the kind of lead singer I have seen in church after church over all my years of leading music.

The service ran for 2 and a half hours. You were the one who opened praise, and when the congregation started dancing, you were still up there holding that high chorus for them. Then they called you back for the offering song, and to close the service, you led a long, slow worship song that went up a key, and then up another key. By the time the pastor gave the benediction, your voice had already done a full day's work.

And then you walked into the car park and started another shift.

An aunty wanted to greet you, and of course you greeted her well. Someone's birthday came up, so you led the birthday song, and you led it loudly. The generator by the gate was still running, so you had to shout your gist over it. Then the choir coordinator called a "quick meeting", and that quick meeting ended up lasting 40 minutes. By evening you were hoarse, and when you said "good morning" on Monday, it came out sounding like a door that has not seen oil in months.

You prepared for Sunday, and you probably even warmed up before service. The problem is that most of us were never taught how to finish.

That is what I want to talk to you about in this letter.

## Planes don't switch off in the sky

Think about a flight landing at Abuja airport. The pilot does not switch off the engines at 35,000 feet and hope for the best. The descent starts long before you can see the runway. The plane slows down a little at a time and comes lower a little at a time, until the wheels touch the ground gently. When that happens, we clap for the pilot. I have never heard anyone clap for a crash.

Your voice deserves the same courtesy. I call it **the slow landing**. It means taking a few minutes, straight after a heavy session of singing, to bring your voice down from singing work to talking work on purpose, instead of just dropping it from the sky.

<figure><img src="/img/posts/your-voice-needs-a-slow-landing/2.svg" alt="Cartoon: on the left a plane drops straight down and crashes; on the right a plane comes down gradually and lands gently on the runway" width="1000" height="470" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Nobody claps for the crash. Bring your voice down the way the pilot brings the plane down.</figcaption></figure>

Voice teachers have a name for this. They call it a **cool-down**, and the easiest way to understand it is as a warm-up played backwards. A warm-up gently wakes your voice before the heavy load, while a cool-down gently winds it down after the load. Most singers have heard of warming up, but very few of us bother with the second one.

So why does this matter so much? Because your Sunday **vocal load** (the total amount of voice you use in a day) does not end at the benediction. The singing ends, but the talking carries on, and it is often louder. When there is noise around you, like a generator or a crowd of people greeting each other, you raise your voice without noticing you are doing it. Scientists have been studying this for over 100 years, and they call it the Lombard effect. So you were already tired, and now, without knowing it, you are shouting.

If you have read my post on [your breath budget](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-breath-has-a-budget/), you will recognise the pattern. When you spend everything early in the day, the end of the day is what pays for it.

## What a cool-down will and won't do for you

The best-known study on this comes from Kari Ragan, a voice teacher and researcher in the United States. She had 20 classically trained female singers sing for 60 minutes on several days. For one week they did a cool-down after singing, and for another week they did not. When the lab measured their voices, it found no real difference between the 2 weeks. The singers themselves, though, felt a clear difference. They said their voices recovered faster, felt less tired, and warmed up more easily the next day. Out of everything they tried, they rated the straw exercises and the humming as the most helpful.

A smaller US study of 9 trained singers also hinted that the benefit may only show up 12 to 14 hours later, which is roughly the next morning. And in a study of choir singers in Belgium, just 15 minutes of choir singing left them sounding better but feeling more tired. In other words, sounding fine and feeling fine are 2 different things.

So here is what I take from all of this. A cool-down will not make you sound better on Sunday night. It will not fix a song that was set in the wrong key, and it will not cure [a habit of shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/). What singers do report is a voice that feels less used up and bounces back faster. If you talk at work on Monday, sing again at midweek service on Wednesday and lead again next Sunday, I would say that is worth 4 minutes of your time. Just keep in mind that these studies were small and were done mostly with classical singers, so test it on your own voice and see what it does for you.

## Your 4-minute slow landing

What follows is a short version of the kind of routine Ragan tested. Try to do it as soon as you can after your last song. You could do it sitting in your car before you start the engine, or while you are walking to the bus stop. All you need is 4 minutes and a drinking straw, so it is a good idea to keep one in your bag.

**1. Straw slides (about 1 minute).** Put a drinking straw between your lips and make a soft "oo" sound through it. Start on a comfortable middle note and slide down slowly to a comfortable low note, the way a siren winds down. Do this 5 or 6 times, and keep each one gentle.

Singers call this kind of sound a **glide**, which simply means one smooth sound moving through many notes without any breaks.

Because of the straw, this is what voice teachers call a **semi-occluded vocal tract** exercise, or SOVT for short. It is a big name for a simple idea. The straw partly closes your mouth, so the air pushes back a little, and that makes it easier for your **vocal folds** (the pair of small folds of tissue in your voice box that make the sound) to vibrate without much effort. And just in case you can't find a straw after service, no worries. You can still get the job done with a lip trill. Let your lips flap loosely, the way a child makes the sound of a motorbike, and slide down from high to low in the same way.

<figure><img src="/img/posts/your-voice-needs-a-slow-landing/3.svg" alt="Cartoon: a relaxed singer with eyes closed slides an oo sound from high to low through a drinking straw; next to him a singer does the same slide with a lip trill" width="1000" height="470" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The straw is the easy option, and when you forget it, your lips can do the work.</figcaption></figure>

**2. Hum your way home (about 1 minute).** Close your lips, keep your teeth slightly apart and hum a gentle "mm" on 3 notes: do, mi, do. Then start the next set a little lower, and the one after that lower again, as if you were walking your voice down a staircase one step at a time. You should feel a buzz on your lips and around your nose. If you feel a squeeze in your throat instead, it means you are humming too loudly, so make the sound smaller.

**3. Find your talking voice again (30 seconds).** Say "mm-hmm" the way you would when you agree with someone on the phone without thinking about it. That relaxed pitch is close to your natural speaking pitch. Now say "Good afternoon, ma" on that same pitch. After a service full of songs sitting in a high **tessitura** (the part of your range where most of a song's notes sit), a lot of singers carry on talking up there without even realising it.

**4. Protect the next 30 minutes.** The landing will not help you much if you take off again in the car park. Move your conversations away from the generator, and walk up to people instead of shouting at them across the hall. Keep sipping water. And if an aunty asks you to sing "just one small chorus" for her daughter's wedding, it is perfectly fine to tell her you will sing it for her next week.

**What if you have more than one service?** I know a lot of you don't go home after one service. There is a second service, sometimes a third, and often a rehearsal somewhere in between.

In that case, do a 1-minute landing between services, with just the straw slides and one "mm-hmm", and save the full 4 minutes for after your last song of the day.

In between services, please don't switch to whispering to "rest" your voice. I know it feels gentle, but one US study filmed the throats of 100 people with voice problems while they whispered, and about 7 in 10 of them squeezed harder when whispering than when they talked normally. So talk softly in your easy speaking voice instead.

If you lead a choir or a worship team, you can add the straw slides and the humming to the last 2 minutes of rehearsal and do them together. Just make sure you have done them yourself first, so you know exactly what you are asking your team to do.

## Don't make the straw your boss

We singers have a special talent for taking something simple and turning it into a burden.

The slow landing is supposed to feel easy. If you start inspecting every hum, checking whether the buzz is sitting in exactly the right spot and frowning at your straw as if it owes you money, your neck and jaw will start to tighten. At that point, the cool-down has become one more job your voice has to do. Do it the same way you would stretch your legs after a long bus ride from Warri to Abuja. Nobody is watching you and nobody is giving you marks out of 10, so just let your body settle.

<figure><img src="/img/posts/your-voice-needs-a-slow-landing/4.svg" alt="Cartoon: on the left a frowning, sweating singer glares at his straw with strain lines on his neck; on the right a relaxed singer hums through the straw with his eyes closed" width="1000" height="470" loading="lazy"><figcaption>If the cool-down makes you tense, it has become another job. Let it be easy.</figcaption></figure>

It is also not a medical treatment. If your voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, if it hurts when you sing, or if you lose your voice, please see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist. A straw cannot fix something a doctor needs to look at.

## Even Jesus took the team aside

Look at what Jesus did when His disciples came back from a busy stretch of ministry.

> "Come ye yourselves apart into a desert place, and rest a while: for there were many coming and going, and they had no leisure so much as to eat." (Mark 6:31)

"Come ye yourselves apart." He took them away from the crowd, even though serving that crowd was the whole point of the ministry.

"Rest a while." He did not tell them to rest forever, only for a while, and then they would go back to work.

"Many coming and going." If you think about it, that is your church car park after service, where everybody wants a minute of you.

Now read the next 3 verses. The crowd ran ahead of them and got to the place first, so their rest was interrupted that very same day. That is exactly why I want you to take your 4 minutes early, before the crowd finds you.

> **The song ends at the benediction. Your voice's work ends when you land it.**

## Your Monday voice note

This Sunday, put a straw in your bag and do the slow landing after your last song.

Then on Monday morning, before you talk to anyone, record a 20-second voice note on your phone. Say "Good morning, this is my Monday voice," and then hum one gentle slide from high to low. Listen back to it and ask yourself 2 questions. Did your "good morning" come out clean, or did it sound like that door that needs oil? And did the hum slide down smoothly, or did it catch and break somewhere along the way?

<figure><img src="/img/posts/your-voice-needs-a-slow-landing/5.svg" alt="Cartoon: on Monday morning a tired singer croaks his greeting; a singer who did the slow landing greets clearly and smiles" width="1000" height="470" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Monday morning will always tell you how Sunday ended.</figcaption></figure>

Keep that voice note. Next Monday, record another one and listen to the 2 of them one after the other. Which one sounds like a voice that landed? If you want something more than your ears to go by, give your voice a number from 1 (fresh) to 10 (completely finished) every Monday, and after a few Sundays with the slow landing, watch which way that number moves.
