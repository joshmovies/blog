Dear lead singer,

I watched you on Sunday. Not you exactly. You, the one I have seen in many churches since I started leading music in 2009.

The service ran for 2 and a half hours. You opened praise, you held the high chorus while the congregation danced, you came back for the offering song, and you closed with a long, slow worship song that went up a key twice. By the benediction your voice had done a day's work.

Then you walked straight into the car park and did another shift.

An aunty wanted to greet you. Someone's birthday needed singing, so you led it, loudly. The generator was still running near the gate, so you shouted your gist over it. Then the choir coordinator called a "quick meeting" that lasted 40 minutes. By evening you were hoarse, and on Monday morning your "good morning" came out like a door that needs oil.

You prepared for Sunday. You probably warmed up. But most of us were never taught how to finish.

That is what this letter is about.

## Planes don't switch off in the sky

When a flight lands at Abuja airport, the pilot does not switch the engines off at 35,000 feet and hope for the best. The descent starts long before the runway. Speed comes down bit by bit, height comes down bit by bit, and the wheels touch the ground gently. We clap for that pilot. Nobody claps for a crash.

Your voice deserves the same courtesy. I call it **the slow landing**: a few minutes, straight after heavy singing, where you bring your voice down from singing work to talking work on purpose, instead of dropping it from the sky.

Voice teachers call this a **cool-down**. Think of it as a warm-up played backwards. A warm-up wakes the voice up gently before the load. A cool-down winds it down gently after the load. Most singers know the first one. Far fewer bother with the second.

Why does it matter? Because your Sunday **vocal load** (the total amount of voice you use in a day) does not end at the benediction. The singing ends. The talking carries on, often louder. And when there is noise around you, like a generator or a crowd greeting each other, you raise your voice without noticing. Scientists have studied this for over 100 years and call it the Lombard effect. You were tired already, and now you are shouting.

If you have read my post on [your breath budget](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-breath-has-a-budget/), you will recognise the pattern. Spend everything early in the day, and the end of the day pays for it.

## What a cool-down will and won't do for you

The best-known study here comes from Kari Ragan, a voice teacher and researcher in the United States. 20 classically trained female singers sang for 60 minutes on several days. For one week they did a cool-down afterwards; for another week they didn't. The lab measurements showed no real difference. The singers felt a clear one: their voices recovered faster, felt less tired and warmed up more easily the next day. Straw exercises and humming were the ones they rated most helpful.

A smaller US study of 9 trained singers hinted that the benefit may show up 12 to 14 hours later, by the next morning. And even 15 minutes of choir singing left a group of Belgian choir singers sounding better but feeling more tired. Sounding fine and feeling fine are 2 different reports.

Here is what I take from it. A cool-down will not make you sound better on Sunday night. It will not fix a song set in the wrong key, or [a habit of shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/). What singers report is a voice that feels less used up and bounces back faster. For a singer who talks at work on Monday, sings again on Wednesday and leads again next Sunday, that is worth 4 minutes. The studies were small and mostly classical singers, so test it on your own voice.

## Your 4-minute slow landing

This is a short version of the kind of routine Ragan tested. Do it as soon as you can after the last song: in your car before you start the engine, or on the walk to the bus stop. You need 4 minutes and a drinking straw. Keep one in your bag.

**1. Straw slides (about 1 minute).** Put a drinking straw between your lips and make a soft "oo" through it. Start on a comfortable middle note and slide down to a comfortable low note, like a siren winding down. Do it 5 or 6 times, each one gentle.

Singers call this a **glide**: one smooth sound moving through many notes with no breaks.

The straw makes it a **semi-occluded vocal tract** exercise, SOVT for short. Big name, simple idea. The straw partly closes your mouth, the air pushes back a little, and your **vocal folds** (the pair of small folds of tissue in your voice box that make the sound) can vibrate with less effort. No straw? A lip trill does the same job: let your lips flap like a child imitating a motorbike while you slide down.

**2. Hum home (about 1 minute).** Close your lips, keep your teeth apart and hum a gentle "mm" on 3 notes: do, mi, do. Start the next one a little lower, then lower again, like walking your voice down the stairs. You should feel a buzz on your lips and around your nose. Squeezing in your throat means you are humming too loudly, so make it smaller.

**3. Find your talking voice (30 seconds).** Say "mm-hmm" the way you agree with someone on the phone, without thinking. That relaxed pitch is close to your natural speaking pitch. Now say "Good afternoon, ma" on that same pitch. After a service full of songs in a high **tessitura** (the part of your range where most of a song's notes sit), many singers keep talking up there without knowing it.

**4. Guard the next 30 minutes.** The landing is useless if you take off again in the car park. Move your conversations away from the generator, and walk to people instead of shouting across the hall. Sip water. If the aunty asks you to sing "just one small chorus" for her daughter's wedding, you are allowed to sing it next week.

**More than one service?** I know many of you don't go home after one service. There is a second service, sometimes a third, and a rehearsal in between.

Do a 1-minute landing between services: just the straw slides and one "mm-hmm". Save the full 4 minutes for after your last song of the day.

And in between, don't whisper to "rest" your voice. It feels gentle, but one US study filmed the throats of 100 people with voice problems while they whispered, and about 7 in 10 squeezed harder than when they talked. Talk softly in your easy speaking voice instead.

If you lead a choir or worship team, add the straw slides and the humming to the last 2 minutes of rehearsal and do them together. Do them yourself first, so you know what you are asking for.

## Don't make the straw your boss

Singers are very good at taking a simple thing and turning it into a burden.

The slow landing is supposed to feel easy. If you start inspecting every hum, checking whether the buzz is in the right spot, frowning at your straw like it owes you money, your neck and jaw will tighten. Then the cool-down becomes one more job your voice has to do. Do it the way you would stretch after a long bus ride from Warri to Abuja. No audience, no marks out of 10. Just let the body settle.

It is also not a medical treatment. If your voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, if singing hurts, or if you lose your voice, see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist. A straw cannot fix what a doctor needs to see.

## Even Jesus took the team aside

Look at what happened when Jesus' disciples came back from a busy stretch of ministry.

> "Come ye yourselves apart into a desert place, and rest a while: for there were many coming and going, and they had no leisure so much as to eat." (Mark 6:31)

"Come ye yourselves apart." He took them away from the crowd, even though the crowd was the ministry.

"Rest a while." Not forever. A while.

"Many coming and going." That is your car park after service. Everyone wants a minute of you.

Now read the next 3 verses. The crowd ran ahead and got there first, and the rest was interrupted that same day. So take your 4 minutes early, before the crowd finds you.

> **The song ends at the benediction. Your voice's work ends when you land it.**

## Your Monday voice note

This Sunday, put a straw in your bag and do the slow landing after your last song.

On Monday morning, before you talk to anyone, record a 20-second voice note on your phone. Say "Good morning, this is my Monday voice," then hum one gentle slide from high to low. Listen back and answer 2 questions. Did the "good morning" come out clean, or did it need oil? Did the hum slide smoothly, or did it catch and break?

Keep that voice note. Next Monday, record again and listen to both, one after the other. Which one sounds like a voice that landed? If you want more than your ears, write down a number from 1 (fresh) to 10 (finished) for how your voice feels each Monday, and watch which way it moves after a few Sundays with the landing.
