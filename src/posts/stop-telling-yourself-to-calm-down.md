"Just calm down. You'll be fine."

Somebody has whispered that to you before. Maybe it was the choir mistress as she pressed the microphone into your hand, or the keyboardist who noticed your fingers shaking during the offering. Maybe it was your own voice inside your head, repeating it over and over like a prayer you didn't fully believe.

And how did it go? I'm guessing your heart did not receive the message. For many of us it runs even faster, because now we are nervous and we are also annoyed with ourselves for not being calm.

So I want to ask you to try something that may sound strange. Stop telling yourself to calm down before you sing. There is a better sentence, it costs nothing, and you can start using it this Sunday. Let me take the questions singers usually ask about it, one by one.

## Why doesn't "calm down" work?

Think about what your body does in the few minutes before you sing. Your heart beats faster, your breathing climbs up into your chest, your mouth goes dry, your hands go cold, and sometimes your knees join the choir uninvited. Psychologists call this state arousal, which simply means your body has switched itself on and is getting ready to act.

Now here is the interesting part. Your body does almost exactly the same thing on your wedding day, or when you are about to open your exam results, or when your team is about to take a penalty in the final. Fear and excitement feel different in the mind, but in the body they look very much alike.

So when you say "calm down" 30 seconds before the intro, you are asking your body to go from full speed to a full stop in no time at all. It is like a generator that has just been pulled into life, roaring away, and you are standing beside it begging it to be quiet. It won't listen, because it has only just started.

That is the idea I want you to hold on to, and I call it **the same-engine rule**: fear and excitement run on the same engine, and you get to choose which name you give it. You cannot switch that engine off a few minutes before you sing, but you can decide where it drives.

## So what do I say instead?

You say "I'm excited." Say it out loud if you can, or under your breath if the pastor is still preaching and you don't want the usher looking at you. Psychologists call this arousal reappraisal, which is a long way of saying you give the racing heart a new name.

The sentence is honest, because your body really is in the same switched-on state that excitement lives in, and the sentence gives that energy a job. A nervous mind looks at everything that could go wrong, like the high note cracking or the faces watching you. An excited mind looks at what could go right, like the moment the whole church joins you on the chorus.

In the Lead Singer's Handbook I wrote that "it's okay to be nervous, but learn to speak to yourself in those moments, especially before the ministration." This sentence is one way to do that speaking. To make it stronger, add your reason: "I'm excited, because I get to lead God's people in this song." Before a slow, deep worship song where "excited" feels like the wrong word, "I'm ready" or "I'm switched on" does the same job.

David knew that kind of heat inside him.

> "My heart was hot within me, while I was musing the fire burned: then spake I with my tongue." (Psalm 39:3)

David was not having a nice day in that psalm. He was troubled, he had been holding his tongue, and the fire inside him was not a comfortable feeling.

But look at what he did with it. "Then spake I with my tongue." The heat did not stay in his chest and choke him. It came out as words, and he took those words straight to God, which is what I want for you on Sunday when the fire comes out as song.

> **You can't switch the engine off before you sing. You can only decide where it drives.**

## Does it really work, or is this just hype?

There is real research behind it. In 2014, Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard asked 113 people to sing a well-known rock song on a karaoke video game that scored their pitch, rhythm and volume. Before singing, each person said one sentence out loud. Those who said "I am excited" scored about 80% on average, those who said "I am calm" scored about 69%, and those who said "I am anxious" scored about 53%.

That was one song on a video game in America, so it needed checking. In 2024, researchers pooled the randomised trials on this kind of reappraisal and found a small but real improvement in performance, slightly bigger for performing in front of people, and they were clear that it is not a silver bullet. I agree with them, so expect a small edge on the day rather than a miracle.

Confidence itself is built over weeks. Researchers who looked at 40 studies on musicians' nerves found that the programmes that mostly helped were things people kept doing for weeks, like talking therapies, mindfulness and yoga, and courses that combined several skills. One of those studies was here in Nigeria, where 70 music undergraduates who made music together in groups for 8 weeks, with breathing and stretching, ended up less anxious than students still waiting their turn.

## "But I'm not excited. I'm terrified."

I hear you, and you don't need to believe the sentence 100% for it to help. Nobody is asking you to flip from terror to joy in one second. You are steering, and even moving from "this is danger" to "this is energy" is a win.

It also helps to know what nerves do to your sound, so you don't panic when you hear it. In one survey of 186 classical music students, singers, along with wind players, had the most nerve complaints in the breath, the mouth and the throat. Your breathing may climb high into your chest and shoulders, which teachers call clavicular breathing, and that leaves you less control over the air.

That shake you hear on a long note when you are nervous is not your vibrato misbehaving. Vibrato is the steady, even pulse a free voice makes on a sustained note, about 5 to 7 times a second. The nervous shake is uneven, and it settles as your breathing settles.

There is one more danger, and it comes from the excitement itself. When you are excited, it is very easy to start pushing, so you sing louder than the song needs, your high notes creep sharp, and before you know it you are rushing ahead of the tempo and pulling the band along with you. So aim the energy at the words and at the people in front of you, not at volume, and keep your ear on the keyboardist so the song stays at the speed you rehearsed.

If your fear goes far beyond Sunday nerves, and it stops you from singing at all, gives you panic attacks, or follows you into the rest of your week, that is more than a sentence can carry. Talk to a qualified counsellor or psychologist. There is no shame in it, and it does not make you a weaker minister.

## What do I do in the 10 seconds before I sing?

Here is a short routine for the choir soloist waiting in the stand and the lead singer walking up during the intro.

**1. Breathe out first.** Before you take a breath in, let a long, slow breath out, the way you would blow on a hot spoon of pepper soup. This settles your breathing without fighting the energy, and then the next breath can drop in low without your shoulders lifting. I wrote more about this in [Your breath has a budget](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-breath-has-a-budget/).

**2. Say the sentence.** "I'm excited, because..." and finish it with your own reason.

**3. Hear your first line before you sing it.** Musicians call this audiation, which means hearing the music clearly in your head, including the very first note. When you can hear the note before you sing it, you are far less likely to scoop up to it or guess at it.

Then you sing, with your attention on the song and on the people rather than on yourself. If the nerves spike again after the first phrase (the group of words you sing on one breath), use the next breath to breathe out a little longer and carry on. And if something goes wrong, the post on [what your face does after a mistake](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/your-face-is-the-second-mistake/) will help with what comes next.

Practise these 10 seconds at home until they need no thinking, because if you are counting breaths and checking your shoulders during the intro, you will sing like a stiff robot.

If you lead a worship team, you can say the sentence together in the prayer before service. But your first job is your own heart, because your team will read your face long before they hear your sentence.

## How will I know it's working?

You can test this at home, because pressing record on your phone puts a little pressure on most of us. Pick the first verse of a song you will sing this Sunday, and before you press record, ask yourself how nervous you feel on a scale of 1 to 10. Then do your 10 seconds, sing, and afterwards ask how up for it you felt while singing, again from 1 to 10. Write both numbers down, and do it 3 times this week.

On Sunday, after the service, write the same 2 numbers in your phone notes, and keep doing it for 4 Sundays.

When you look back, don't be surprised if the nerves number hardly moves, because the engine is supposed to keep running. The number to watch is the second one, and alongside it, the first line of your first recording and your latest one played back to back, where you can hear whether the long notes have stopped shaking.

This Sunday, when your heart starts racing during the song before yours, don't fight it. Tell yourself you're excited and why, then take a moment to hear your first note before you open your mouth.
