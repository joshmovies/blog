In 2025, Spotify paid Nigerian artists just over ₦60 billion.

That is a big number, and it made the news in March. Then TechCabal did a simple division. ₦60 billion, shared across 30.3 billion streams, comes to just under ₦2 per stream.

₦2. 10 plays of your song earn about ₦20, and a sachet of pure water in Lagos cost ₦25 to ₦40 this year.

I am not writing this to discourage anyone from releasing music. I want you to release it with your eyes open. Many singers carry a quiet picture in their heads: the song drops, the streams climb, and the money starts to come in. That picture rests on 4 beliefs, and each one is only partly true.

Before we take them one by one, here is the tool you should leave with: **the stream sum**. Before you plan around streaming income, you work out how many streams that income needs. It takes 2 minutes and a calculator.

One note first. Everything below is about Spotify, because Spotify publishes Nigerian figures. Audiomack, Boomplay and YouTube pay in their own ways, so check each one separately.

## "Spotify pays a fixed amount per stream"

It does not. Spotify says so plainly on its own artist support pages: it does not pay royalties by a per-play or per-stream rate.

Here is how it works instead. Each month, Spotify takes the money it collected from subscriptions and adverts, removes things like taxes and card fees, and puts the rest in a pool. Each rightsholder gets a share of that pool equal to their share of all the streams.

Spotify pays this to whoever holds the rights to the recording, through a record label or, for most independent singers, a distributor: the service that put your song on Spotify and passes the money on to you. Spotify calls this system streamshare.

At a family reunion, one big pot of soup is cooked and shared by how many people each household brought. Your plate depends on 2 things you don't control: how big the pot was, and how many people everyone else brought. A crowded reunion does not mean more soup for your house.

In Nigeria, the pot is small. TechCabal puts a Premium plan here at about ₦1,600 a month, against roughly $13.78 in Sweden.

By its estimate, 1 million streams from listeners in Nigeria earn about $300, while the same streams in Sweden can earn up to $10,000. At the exchange rate behind TechCabal's own figures (about ₦1,370 to the dollar), that is roughly ₦0.40 per stream at home. These are TechCabal's calculations from Spotify's report, not rates Spotify publishes, and they move with the naira. The direction is still clear: where your listeners live matters as much as how many there are.

So ₦2 is an average across every Nigerian artist and every listener, at home and abroad. Your own number could be higher or lower. The only way to know it is the earnings page your distributor gives you.

## "Every stream earns something"

This one surprises people.

Since April 2024, a track must reach at least 1,000 streams in the previous 12 months before it earns any recording royalties on Spotify. There is also a minimum number of different listeners, which Spotify does not publish, to stop people gaming the system. Below that line, the track earns nothing from the pool.

Spotify's explanation is that tracks with fewer than 1,000 streams a year were earning about $0.03 a month on average. That money now goes to the tracks that do qualify.

Now take a typical church release. You record a song, share it in the choir WhatsApp group, and 300 friends play it 2 or 3 times each. That is about 750 streams. Your household came to the reunion, but there were too few of you to be served.

This rule covers recording royalties only. Songwriting royalties, the money owed to whoever wrote the song, are paid separately through publishers and collecting societies, and the threshold does not apply to them. If you write your own songs, that second source of money is worth learning about.

## "One good song can carry me"

Now let us do the stream sum.

Say you want ₦100,000 a month from Spotify. At the national average of about ₦2 per stream, you need roughly 50,000 streams every month. If most of your listeners are in Nigeria, at about ₦0.40, you need closer to 250,000. And that is before your distributor takes its fee or its percentage.

To make that real: for a church of 500 members to give you 250,000 streams, each member would need to play your song about 17 times a day, every day of the month.

Jesus gave a builder's test that fits here:

> "For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?" (Luke 14:28)

Intending to build. The plan is good. Nobody is mocked for wanting a tower.

Sitteth not down first. The counting happens before the building, not after the foundation is laid.

Counteth the cost. You do the actual sum, with actual numbers.

Whether he have sufficient to finish it. A wise builder asks "Can I finish?" before he asks "Can I start?" A song you can afford to record but cannot promote, or promote once but cannot keep alive, is a foundation with no walls.

> **Count the streams before you count the money.**

## "Streaming is where the money is"

For most Nigerian artists, most of the money is somewhere else, at least for now.

A December 2025 market report by the National Council for Arts and Culture and RegalStone Capital, called Basslines to Billions, estimated the Nigerian music industry at about $600 million (around ₦901 billion) a year. According to Nairametrics' coverage, the report put live performances at about 66% of artist earnings, and streaming and other digital platforms at about 30% of industry revenue. Osita Ugeh of Duke Concept Entertainment, quoted in the same coverage, gave a rougher split: about 60% from live shows, 20% from streaming and 5 to 10% from brand deals.

Treat those as estimates. They come from industry data and interviews, they mix 2024 and 2025, and they mostly describe secular artists. Gospel music has its own economy of ministrations, conferences and church programmes that nobody has measured well. But the pattern points the same way: the people who pay most for music are the ones in the room.

To be fair to streaming, it is not small. Payouts to Nigerian artists grew more than 140% in naira terms over 2 years, though growth slowed to about 3% between 2024 and 2025. About 58% of that money went to independent artists and labels, people without a big record deal behind them.

Local listening to Nigerian artists also rose about 170% in a year, and Nigerian acts held over 80% of the Spotify Nigeria daily Top 50. Streaming is how a stranger in another city finds you. That stranger may be the one who later invites you to minister.

So keep a second question next to "Will streaming pay me?" Ask "What is streaming doing for me?" For many of us, its biggest job is getting us heard by the people who will pay in other ways.

## Where the gift meets the sum

In my confidence guide I wrote about a society that sells us, from early on, the idea that our gift is a hobby and not something a livelihood can be built on. I still believe that idea is wrong.

The other side matters too. Throwing out that old lie should not lead us to a new one, that a single upload will feed us. A gift can support a life. It usually does so through more than one door: live ministry, teaching and coaching, songwriting, products, and streaming. The singers who last are usually the ones who know which door pays what.

Ask yourself honestly: when you imagine your music paying, which door are you picturing? And have you ever counted what comes through it?

## Before you release your next song

Sit down with your phone calculator tonight and answer these 5 questions. Write the answers down.

1. **What monthly amount do you want from Spotify?** Write the naira figure.
2. **Divide it by 2.** That is roughly how many streams a month it needs at the national average. If most of your listeners are in Nigeria, divide the original figure by 0.4 instead.
3. **Check your last release.** Did each track pass 1,000 streams in the last 12 months? If you don't know, log in to your distributor's account and find out.
4. **Find your real rate.** Divide what your distributor paid you by the streams it reports. Compare it with ₦2 and ₦0.40.
5. **Name your other doors.** List every way your singing has brought in money or invitations in the last year, and which of them came because someone first heard you online.

If the answer to question 2 frightens you, good. Keep releasing, with a plan for the other doors, and count the cost before you lay the foundation.
