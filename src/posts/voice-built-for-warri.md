In 2017 I moved from Warri to Abuja. I had basically spent most of my life in Warri and done most of my music there. I had gone out many times to different states and places for several musical engagements, and I had even served in the north. But living somewhere is not the same as visiting.

In Abuja I discovered something I had not experienced before. My voice began to dry out and break, or "crack", more than usual. It happened so frequently that it became a major concern for me, so much that I had to consult an acclaimed vocal coach for help.

After the session I understood what had happened. I had been greatly affected by the change in climate that came with the change in environment.

You see, the south is hot and humid, and I had developed my voice in that climate. Plus, we didn't really turn up the AC that much in our music meetings and services.

But Abuja is much drier, especially from November to March, and here they turn the AC down to freezing point! And the harmattan here? Ah! It was another level entirely. So every time I got on a stage to sing, I was experiencing a double-barrel vocal invasion: the natural climate outside and the ACs inside.

The result was many terrible ministrations and performances, until I met my coach. She saved my ministry. Sometimes you get frustrated, because you know in your head what you are capable of, but your vocal folds keep saying no and "folding up" like an intimidated child. (Your vocal folds are the 2 small folds of tissue in your larynx, your voice box, whose vibration makes the sound.)

She made me understand how changes in environment, and several other factors, affect our voices and our performance. She identified the climate factors. She explained how the seasons of the body, like pregnancy in women, affect singers. And she corrected a posture flaw I had developed, and mastered, over 20 years of ministering.

## Why Warri spoiled my voice

Let me put numbers to "hot and humid". Humidity is the amount of moisture in the air. In Warri it sits around 80% even in December. In January, Abuja's average drops to about 44%, and on a hard harmattan day it falls much lower.

So for years my voice had been rehearsing in something close to a steam room without knowing it. Then I moved it into a desert season.

Picture a Warri boy who travels to Abuja in December with only singlets in his bag. Nobody needs to explain anything to him on the first morning. His body explains it. My voice got the same lecture.

That is the lesson I took from my coach, and I call it **the home-climate rule: your voice was trained by the air it grew up in. When the air changes, your habits must change with it.**

The AC made it worse. It adds no moisture back to dry air, and a cold blast across your face dries your throat even faster. How much does dry air matter? In one American study, 34 trained sopranos breathed very dry air through the mouth for just 15 minutes. Afterwards, making sound felt like harder work, and without treatment that feeling lasted at least 2 hours (Tanner and colleagues, 2010). It was a lab test with extreme air, so don't stretch it. But it matches what many of us feel after a long service in a cold, dry hall.

## What she found in me

The weather was only half of it. The other half was me. Here is what my coach found, in plain words.

### 1. My head went up on high notes

I had an overly raised head posture. I was almost always bending backwards when I sang higher notes, forcing around the vocal area and constricting the muscles of my neck.

Those neck muscles have a name: the extrinsic laryngeal muscles. Their job is to hold the voice box in place, not to sing. Voice clinics often see them gripping in people whose voices are strained (Stepp and colleagues, 2010). When they grip, the small muscles inside the larynx that tune your pitch lose their freedom.

A small lift of the head on high notes is common, even among trained singers. One study of university voice students found their heads rose a little as the pitch rose. My problem was the extreme: head thrown back, neck stretched and tight.

### 2. Then it went down

On other lines I did the opposite. I pushed down on my vocal folds by putting my head down, chin towards my chest, as if I could force the note out by leaning on it. Same tension, different direction.

Here is how to check yourself when nobody is watching:

1. Prop your phone at shoulder height, to your side, so it films you from the side. Record yourself singing the highest phrase (one stretch of melody between breaths) of a song you minister often.
2. Watch it back with the sound off. Does your chin climb as the melody climbs? Does it dig into your chest on the strong lines?
3. Rest your fingertips lightly on the soft spot under your jaw and sing the phrase again. That spot is where your tongue root sits. If it bulges hard into your fingers, your tongue is helping to push.
4. Sing it once more with your chin level, the back of your neck long and your eyes on the back wall.

One warning. Don't turn yourself into a soldier on parade. If you go stiff trying to hold the "right" posture, you have only swapped one tension for another.

### 3. I kept singing excessively high notes

Even after the switch, I maintained the same habit of singing excessively high notes. My voice was already struggling, and I was still asking it for the top of my range.

Every song has a tessitura: the part of your range where most of its notes sit. A song that keeps you up there for a long time is very different from one that touches one high note and comes back down. When your voice is dry and tired, a high song in the old key is like asking a car with a weak battery to tow another car.

So in the dry season, take songs down. Even one tone, 2 keys down on the keyboard, changes the whole song. Tell your keyboardist before rehearsal, not in the middle of the song. If high notes feel like a fight all year round, [Stop shouting your high notes](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/stop-shouting-your-high-notes/) is for you.

### 4. I didn't hydrate, and I didn't steam

I was not a very good fan of hydrating and lubricating my voice. And I didn't do any steaming under those conditions. In harmattan and cold AC, that is like driving through the desert with no water in the radiator. I wrote about both in [Your voice in harmattan](https://blog.joshuaediale.com/posts/harmattan-voice-sunday-plan/), so I won't repeat it all here.

## The seasons of the body

**Just a quick one for pregnant female vocalists.** Pregnancy hormones can make your vocal folds swell a little. Reflux often gets worse, and your lungs have less room as the baby grows. In one singer's case, researchers found she needed more breath pressure just to get her folds vibrating while she was pregnant (Lã and Sundberg, 2009).

So lower the key when you need to, rest more, and don't judge your voice against your old recordings. A sudden change in your voice, pain when you sing or a big loss of range means see an ENT specialist, not push harder.

**What you eat, and when.** Stomach acid can travel up and irritate the larynx. Doctors call this laryngopharyngeal reflux, and singers seem to get it more than most people. BAPAM, the UK's body for performers' health, points at late meals after evening events.

Its advice: eat smaller meals, and eat them earlier. Cut down on coffee, strong tea and energy drinks (the caffeine in them can trigger reflux), and soft drinks like Coke, Fanta and Sprite. Go easy on fried, greasy food and on very peppery food.

And alcohol? If you still need to be told, as a singer or minister, to stop taking alcohol, then there's a serious problem.

That suya and pepper soup at midnight after Saturday's programme is not your voice's friend on Sunday morning.

Solomon put the whole matter in one line:

> "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven." (Ecclesiastes 3:1)

"To every thing." That includes your voice. "A season." Seasons are expected. They are not proof that something is wrong with you. The wise singer does not argue with the season. He prepares for it.

## Your dry-season checklist

Before your next ministration:

- Where will you stand? If the AC blows straight at you, move, or ask for the temperature to come up.
- Drink through the day before and the morning of. Steam early, at home.
- Record yourself singing your highest phrase from the side. What did you observe? Was your chin level? Was your neck free?
- Check your keys. If a song sits high and your voice feels dry, take it down a tone this month.
- Finish your last heavy meal at least 3 hours before you sleep.

Then keep score. After each service this month, write down how many times your voice cracked or you had to clear your throat. By the end of December, that number will tell you whether your voice has learned the new climate.

If your voice stays hoarse for more than 2 to 3 weeks, or singing hurts, see a doctor, ideally an ENT specialist.

My voice was built in Warri. It took a coach, and many terrible ministrations, to teach it how to live in Abuja. Wherever your voice was built, teach it where it lives now.
